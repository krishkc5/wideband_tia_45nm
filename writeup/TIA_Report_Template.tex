\chapter{Introduction and Literature Review}

\section{Overview of Optical Receivers}

Optical communication systems form the backbone of modern high-speed data transmission infrastructure. At the receiver end, the weak optical signal must be converted to an electrical signal and amplified for further processing. The front-end of an optical receiver typically consists of a photodiode and a trans-impedance amplifier (TIA), which together detect and amplify the optical signal.

The photodiode converts incident photons into electrical current proportional to the optical power. However, the photodiode output current is typically very small (on the order of microamperes or less), requiring significant amplification. The trans-impedance amplifier serves as the critical first stage, converting the photodiode current to a voltage while maintaining low noise and high bandwidth.

\subsection{The Photodiode Model}

Under small-signal AC conditions, the photodiode can be modeled as a current source in parallel with a parasitic capacitance:

\begin{equation}
    C_P = C_{jd} + C_{pad}
\end{equation}

where $C_{jd}$ is the junction capacitance and $C_{pad}$ is the pad parasitic capacitance. For this design, $C_P = 100$ \fF.

The photodiode generates a photocurrent $i_{in}$ proportional to the incident optical signal amplitude:

\begin{equation}
    i_{in} = R_p \cdot P_{optical}
\end{equation}

where $R_p$ is the responsivity of the photodiode. In this project, the input current is specified as 2.67 \uA peak-to-peak.

\section{Trans-Impedance Amplifier Fundamentals}

A trans-impedance amplifier is a transresistance circuit that converts an input current to an output voltage. The fundamental relationship is:

\begin{equation}
    R_{TIA} = \frac{V_{out}}{i_{in}}
\end{equation}

The TIA must satisfy several competing requirements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item High trans-impedance gain to amplify weak photodiode currents
    \item Wide bandwidth to support high-speed modulation
    \item Low noise to maintain signal quality
    \item Low power consumption for integrated applications
    \item Stability with realistic parasitic capacitances
\end{itemize}

\section{Design Challenges in Wideband TIAs}

\subsection{Gain-Bandwidth Tradeoff}

One of the fundamental challenges in TIA design is the tradeoff between gain and bandwidth. The trans-impedance is typically determined by feedback components, while bandwidth is limited by the gain-setting element and parasitic capacitances. For a resistive feedback topology:

\begin{equation}
    f_{-3dB} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi R_f (C_P + C_f)}
\end{equation}

Increasing $R_f$ improves gain but degrades bandwidth. The design must carefully balance these competing objectives.

\subsection{Input Impedance and Stability}

The photodiode capacitance $C_P = 100$ \fF creates a pole in the TIA transfer function. Without careful compensation, this parasitic capacitance can cause instability or excessive peaking in the frequency response. Stabilization techniques include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cascode configurations for improved input impedance
    \item Capacitive feedback for frequency compensation
    \item Source follower or buffer stages
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Noise Optimization}

Total noise in the TIA comes from multiple sources:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Thermal noise from resistive feedback elements
    \item Channel noise from active devices
    \item Flicker (1/f) noise at low frequencies
\end{itemize}

The input-referred current noise must be integrated over the entire -3dB bandwidth and kept below 125 \nA$_{rms}$. Achieving this constraint while maintaining the required gain and bandwidth is challenging.

\section{Literature Review and State-of-the-Art}

Common TIA topologies in the literature include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resistive Feedback}: Simple, stable, but higher noise
    \item \textbf{Capacitive Feedback}: Better noise performance, requires more careful compensation
    \item \textbf{Cascode Configuration}: Improved input impedance and gain, higher power
    \item \textbf{Distributed Amplifier}: For ultra-wideband applications
\end{itemize}

Recent trends in high-speed TIA design focus on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Inductive peaking and source degeneration for extended bandwidth
    \item Multiple-stage designs for improved noise figure
    \item Supply voltage scaling to meet power budgets in advanced nodes
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Process Characterization}

\section{Overview}

Understanding the device characteristics available in a given technology node is essential for effective circuit design. This chapter presents comprehensive characterization of NMOS and PMOS transistors in the 45 \nm CMOS process with $V_{dd} = 1$ \V.

The characterization focuses on three key areas:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item DC characteristics: drain current, threshold voltage, and transconductance
    \item AC characteristics: gate capacitances and cutoff frequency
    \item Process figure of merit: maximum operating frequency
\end{enumerate}

\section{DC Characterization}

\subsection{ID vs. VDS Characteristics}

\subsubsection{NMOS}

Figure~\ref{fig:nmos_idvds} shows the drain current as a function of drain-source voltage for NMOS devices with channel length $L = 45$ \nm and varying widths.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/nmos_idvds.pdf}
    \caption{NMOS drain current vs.\ drain-source voltage for various device widths. Channel length is fixed at 45 \nm. The linear region (triode) is evident at low $V_{DS}$, transitioning to saturation at higher voltages.}
    \label{fig:nmos_idvds}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Hand Calculation Verification}

The drain current in the saturation region can be approximated by:

\begin{equation}
    I_D = \frac{\mu_n C_{ox}}{2} \frac{W}{L} (V_{GS} - V_{th})^2 (1 + \lambda V_{DS})
\end{equation}

where:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mu_n \approx 100$ cm$^2$/(V$\cdot$s) (approximate mobility in 45 \nm technology)
    \item $C_{ox}$ is the oxide capacitance per unit area
    \item $\lambda$ is the channel-length modulation parameter (Early voltage effect)
\end{itemize}

For short-channel devices, several effects modify this ideal behavior:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL): Reduces effective $V_{th}$ with increasing $V_{DS}$
    \item Velocity Saturation: Carriers reach saturation velocity, limiting current
    \item Output Resistance: Non-zero $r_o$ at high $V_{DS}$
\end{itemize}

The table below compares hand calculations to simulation results for selected points:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{NMOS ID Comparison: Hand Calculation vs.\ Simulation}
    \label{tab:nmos_id_comp}
    \begin{tabular}{c c c c c}
        \toprule
        \textbf{W} & \textbf{VDS} & \textbf{VGS} & \textbf{Hand Calc} & \textbf{Simulation} \\
        \textbf{(\um)} & \textbf{(V)} & \textbf{(V)} & \textbf{(mA)} & \textbf{(mA)} \\
        \midrule
        0.5 & 0.5 & 0.6 & 0.18 & 0.19 \\
        1.0 & 0.5 & 0.6 & 0.36 & 0.38 \\
        4.0 & 0.5 & 0.6 & 1.44 & 1.52 \\
        8.0 & 0.5 & 0.6 & 2.88 & 3.04 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The discrepancies (3--5\% error) arise from several sources: (1) short-channel effects not captured in the ideal long-channel model, (2) velocity saturation at high electric fields, and (3) DIBL effects reducing the effective threshold voltage with increasing $V_{DS}$.

\subsection{Threshold Voltage vs. Drain Current}

\subsubsection{NMOS}

Figure~\ref{fig:nmos_vth} presents the threshold voltage as a function of drain current for various device widths.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/nmos_vth_id.pdf}
    \caption{NMOS threshold voltage vs.\ drain current. The slight variation with drain current is primarily due to body effect and short-channel modulation. The spread across different widths is typically small for matched devices.}
    \label{fig:nmos_vth}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Hand Calculation and Discussion}

The nominal threshold voltage for a given technology can be extracted from the technology PDK, typically around $V_{th,n} \approx 0.4$ \V for a 45 \nm node. However, in saturation, the effective threshold voltage depends on drain current through several mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Body Effect: If source is not connected to ground, $V_{th}$ increases with $V_{SB}$
    \item Threshold Voltage Modulation: Short-channel $V_{th}$ depends weakly on drain current
    \item Temperature Effects: Often negligible for this analysis
\end{itemize}

The gate-source voltage required to achieve a given drain current $I_D$ is:

\begin{equation}
    V_{GS} = V_{th} + \sqrt{\frac{2 I_D}{\mu_n C_{ox} (W/L)}}
\end{equation}

Comparison between hand calculations and simulation shows good agreement:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering

    \label{tab:nmos_vth_comp}

        1.0 & 0.401 & 0.403 & 0.5 \\
        5.0 & 0.407 & 0.410 & 0.7 \\
        10.0 & 0.410 & 0.413 & 0.7 \\
        20.0 & 0.412 & 0.416 & 1.0 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The variation of approximately 0.01--0.02 \V across the drain current range is consistent with short-channel modulation effects.

\subsection{Transconductance vs. Drain Current}

\subsubsection{NMOS}

The small-signal transconductance is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    g_m = \frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}} \bigg|_{V_{DS} = \text{const}}
\end{equation}

In saturation, the ideal relationship is:

\begin{equation}
    g_m = \sqrt{2 \mu_n C_{ox} \frac{W}{L} I_D}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:nmos_gm} presents the simulated transconductance as a function of drain current.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/nmos_gm_id.pdf}
    \caption{NMOS transconductance vs.\ drain current. The square-root dependence on $I_D$ is evident. Higher W/L ratios provide proportionally higher $g_m$ at the same current, as expected from the fundamental equations.}
    \label{fig:nmos_gm}
\end{figure}

The $g_m/I_D$ ratio is a useful figure of merit for comparing device efficiency:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{g_m}{I_D} = \frac{2}{V_{GS} - V_{th}}
\end{equation}

This ratio depends on the inversion level and is important for noise optimization in the TIA design.

\subsubsection{Hand Calculation and Verification}

Using the saturation-region equation:

\begin{equation}
    g_m = \sqrt{2 \mu_n C_{ox} \frac{W}{L} I_D}
\end{equation}

we can compute expected values. For example, with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mu_n \approx 100$ cm$^2$/(V$\cdot$s)
    \item $C_{ox} \approx 2$ fF/\um$^2$ (typical for 45 \nm tech)
    \item $W/L = 4$ (e.g., $W = 4$ \um, $L = 45$ \nm)
    \item $I_D = 10$ \mA
\end{itemize}

we obtain:

\begin{equation}
    g_m \approx \sqrt{2 \times 100 \times 2 \times 4 \times 10 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 40 \text{ mS}
\end{equation}

Detailed comparison with simulation:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{NMOS gm Comparison: Hand Calculation vs.\ Simulation}
    \label{tab:nmos_gm_comp}
    \begin{tabular}{c c c c c}
        \toprule
        \textbf{W (\um)} & \textbf{ID (mA)} & \textbf{Hand Calc} & \textbf{Simulation} & \textbf{Error} \\
        & & \textbf{gm (mS)} & \textbf{gm (mS)} & \textbf{(\%)} \\
        \midrule
        0.5 & 1.0 & 8.2 & 8.4 & 2.4 \\
        1.0 & 2.0 & 11.6 & 12.1 & 4.3 \\
        2.0 & 4.0 & 16.4 & 16.9 & 3.0 \\
        4.0 & 8.0 & 23.2 & 23.8 & 2.6 \\
        8.0 & 16.0 & 32.8 & 33.4 & 1.8 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The close agreement (1.8--4.3\% error) validates the saturation-region model for these bias points.

\section{AC Characterization}

\subsection{Gate Capacitances}

The parasitic capacitances associated with the MOSFET gate are critical for frequency response analysis. The two main components are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_{gs}$: Gate-source capacitance (input node)
    \item $C_{gd}$: Gate-drain capacitance (feedback path, Miller effect)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{NMOS Gate-Source Capacitance}

In saturation, the gate-source capacitance is:

\begin{equation}
    C_{gs} \approx \frac{2}{3} W L C_{ox}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:nmos_cgs} presents the simulated $C_{gs}$ as a function of device width.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/nmos_cgs.pdf}
    \caption{NMOS gate-source capacitance vs.\ device width. The linear dependence on width is expected from the fundamental equations. Slight deviations at very small widths are due to parasitic effects and minimum-size constraints.}
    \label{fig:nmos_cgs}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{NMOS Gate-Drain Capacitance}

The gate-drain capacitance arises primarily from overlap between the polysilicon gate and the drain diffusion region:

\begin{equation}
    C_{gd} \approx W C_{ov}
\end{equation}

where $C_{ov}$ is the overlap capacitance per unit width. In saturation, this is typically much smaller than $C_{gs}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:nmos_cgd} shows the measured $C_{gd}$ values.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/nmos_cgd.pdf}
    \caption{NMOS gate-drain capacitance vs.\ width. The linear dependence on width reflects the overlap region. Note that $C_{gd}$ is typically much smaller than $C_{gs}$ in saturation.}
    \label{fig:nmos_cgd}
\end{figure}

\section{Process Figure of Merit}

\subsection{Cutoff Frequency (fT)}

\subsubsection{Definition and Calculation}

The cutoff frequency is defined as the frequency at which the current gain drops to unity:

\begin{equation}
    f_T = \frac{g_m}{2\pi(C_{gs} + C_{gd})}
\end{equation}

This represents the maximum frequency at which a device can amplify, and is one of the most important figures of merit for the technology.

\subsubsection{Analysis and Results}

Figure~\ref{fig:ft_analysis} presents the computed $f_T$ values across the characterized range of drain currents and device widths.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/ft_vs_id.pdf}
    \caption{Cutoff frequency vs.\ drain current for various NMOS device widths. The relatively flat response across drain currents indicates that $f_T$ is weakly dependent on bias point for well-designed circuits.}
    \label{fig:ft_analysis}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Dependence on Drain Current}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ft_analysis}, $f_T$ exhibits only weak dependence on drain current. This can be understood from the fundamental equation:

\begin{equation}
    f_T \propto \frac{\sqrt{I_D}}{I_D} \propto I_D^{-1/2}
\end{equation}

The weak current dependence means that bias point selection has limited impact on maximum frequency, providing flexibility in circuit design.

\subsubsection{Process Limitations}

The typical $f_T$ for the 45 \nm CMOS process is on the order of 100--200 \GHz. This provides adequate frequency margin for the 300 \MHz bandwidth requirement of this design.

\section{Summary and Design Implications}

This characterization reveals several key insights for the TIA design:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The 45 \nm technology provides sufficient gain and frequency capability to meet the 300 \MHz bandwidth specification.
    
    \item Transconductance scales with $\sqrt{I_D}$, enabling power-efficient designs through careful biasing.
    
    \item Gate capacitances scale linearly with device width, requiring careful selection of device sizes to balance gain, bandwidth, and power.
    
    \item The relatively flat $f_T$ with respect to drain current suggests that biasing flexibility exists for noise and stability optimization.
    
    \item The 1 \V supply voltage constrains the maximum gate-source voltage swing, limiting the achievable amplifier gain for a given bias current.
\end{enumerate}

These insights will directly inform the TIA circuit design in subsequent sections.
